# Hypertrophy Coaching Dashboard Context

## Purpose

This document is the source of truth for an HTML dashboard that turns a personalized hypertrophy and body-recomposition plan into a clear weekly operating system. The user should be able to open the page, understand the priorities, complete a workout, and review progress without needing the original conversation.

## User profile

- Male, age 32
- Height: 178 cm
- Starting body weight: 86.1 kg
- Noticeable belly and face/chin fat by self-report; do not infer or display an exact body-fat percentage.
- Intermediate trainee with approximately 1.5 years of recent continuous weight training. Earlier training was intermittent.
- Availability: 3–4 total training days per week, including cardio.
- Typical session limit: about 60 minutes.
- No diagnosed limitations reported. Technically demanding barbell squat and deadlift variations are less preferred when practical; exercise substitutions should be offered.
- Current supplement: BAAM Absolute whey isolate, commonly taken in the morning and after workouts. It is a convenience, not a requirement.
- Nutrition logging is inconsistent, and the user eats out frequently, making exact calorie and macro tracking difficult.
- Typical sleep: 5–7 hours per night, without formal tracking.

## Primary outcome and priorities

The main goal is body recomposition: build or preserve muscle while gradually reducing fat. Do not begin with a traditional calorie-surplus bulk.

Physique priorities, in order of emphasis: chest, back, and shoulders. The plan should still train the lower body and arms for balance and progression.

## Weekly training system

Use an Upper/Lower rotation. A three-day week simply continues the sequence the following week, so no session is permanently skipped.

### Upper A — chest emphasis

1. Bench press or machine chest press — 3 × 6–10
2. Incline dumbbell press — 3 × 8–12
3. Chest-supported row — 3 × 8–12
4. Lat pulldown — 3 × 8–12
5. Cable or machine lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
6. Triceps pressdown — 2 × 10–15
7. Biceps curl — 2 × 10–15

### Lower A

1. Leg press — 3 × 8–12
2. Romanian deadlift, or a hamstring curl/machine alternative — 3 × 8–12
3. Bulgarian split squat, hack squat, or supported split-squat alternative — 2–3 × 8–12 per leg
4. Seated or lying leg curl — 2–3 × 10–15
5. Calf raise — 3 × 10–15
6. Optional core movement — 2–3 controlled sets

### Upper B — back and shoulder emphasis

1. Incline machine or dumbbell press — 3 × 8–12
2. Pull-up or lat pulldown — 3 × 6–12
3. One-arm cable row or machine row — 3 × 8–12
4. Seated dumbbell or machine shoulder press — 3 × 6–10
5. Cable lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
6. Rear-delt fly or face pull — 2–3 × 12–20
7. Optional arms superset — 2 × 10–15 each

### Lower B

1. Hack squat, leg press, or another stable squat-pattern machine — 3 × 8–12
2. Hip thrust or glute bridge — 3 × 8–12
3. Leg extension — 2–3 × 10–15
4. Leg curl — 3 × 10–15
5. Calf raise — 3 × 10–15
6. Optional core movement — 2–3 controlled sets

### Effort, rest, and progression

- Most working sets should finish with approximately 1–3 reps in reserve (RIR): the estimated number of good-form repetitions still possible.
- Avoid taking every set to failure. Near-failure effort is useful, but technique and recoverability come first.
- Rest about 2–3 minutes for demanding compound movements and 60–120 seconds for isolation work; stay within the 60-minute session limit.
- Use double progression: keep the same weight until all prescribed sets reach the top of the rep range with good form and the intended RIR, then increase the load slightly and build reps again.
- Log weight, reps, and RIR. If performance stalls, first check sleep, food, technique, and fatigue before adding volume.
- Warm up with a few minutes of general movement plus lighter ramp-up sets for the first major exercise. Warm-ups should prepare the body, not exhaust it.

## Cardio placement

Do two 20–30 minute moderate sessions per week. Place them after upper-body sessions or on separate days when possible. Keep intensity sustainable: breathing is elevated, but the session does not compromise the next lower-body workout. Daily walking is a useful optional baseline.

## Nutrition approach

The plan must work even when meals are eaten out and calories are imprecise.

- Anchor each meal around a clear protein source: lean meat, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt, tofu, or a whey shake.
- Aim for roughly 3–5 protein feedings across the day instead of trying to make one meal perfect.
- Build restaurant meals around protein plus vegetables or fruit, then choose a sensible carbohydrate and fat portion.
- Prefer grilled, steamed, roasted, or stir-fried options more often than deep-fried meals; sauces and drinks are common hidden calories.
- Use a consistent “default order” at frequently visited restaurants.
- If exact logging is impossible, use portion estimates and focus on the weekly weight trend.
- Whey isolate can fill a protein gap in the morning or after training. Timing is secondary to total daily protein; a post-workout shake is not mandatory if food already covers the target.

## Recovery and DOMS guidance

The user recently experienced leg DOMS lasting about four days after barbell squats. Explain that delayed-onset muscle soreness can occur after unfamiliar, high-volume, or highly lengthened work and is not a direct measure of workout quality.

- Normal DOMS is usually diffuse muscle tenderness and stiffness that gradually improves with light movement.
- For manageable soreness, use walking, gentle mobility, a thorough warm-up, hydration, and normal nutrition. Do not aggressively stretch or “punish” the muscle.
- If soreness is severe, changes movement, or significantly reduces strength, reduce load/sets, choose a stable alternative, and allow more recovery before repeating the movement.
- Stop and seek qualified medical guidance for sharp or localized pain, swelling, bruising, joint pain, numbness, unusual weakness, dark urine, fever, or symptoms that worsen or do not improve.
- Progress lower-body difficulty gradually, especially when reintroducing barbell squats or deadlift variations.

## Progress tracking and adjustment rules

The dashboard should provide a weekly review area for:

- Morning body weight, ideally several readings per week and reviewed as a weekly average
- Waist measurement under consistent conditions
- Progress photos under consistent lighting and pose
- Exercise performance: load, reps, RIR, and whether form was stable
- Approximate protein and calorie adherence
- Sleep duration and perceived recovery
- Soreness, pain flags, and free-form notes

Use trend-based decisions, not single-day changes:

- If the 2–3 week average is not moving and adherence is reasonably consistent, make a small adjustment: slightly reduce intake or add a little low-intensity activity.
- If weight is falling faster than about 0.4 kg/week, performance is declining, or recovery is poor, add a modest amount of food and reassess.
- If waist is slowly decreasing while gym performance is stable or improving, continue even if scale weight is flat.
- Review the plan every 2–3 weeks; do not react to one weigh-in.

## Design and UX requirements

- Make the page feel like a polished personal coaching dashboard, not a long article.
- Prioritize scanability with cards, badges, step sequences, tables, accordions, and clear section labels.
- Use strong visual emphasis for the four numbers the user should remember: ~180 g protein, 2,400–2,500 kcal starting range, 0.2–0.4 kg/week loss, and 3–4 training days.
- Make “today’s action” obvious: choose the next rotation workout, hit the protein anchor, complete cardio if scheduled, and log the review fields.
- Keep all content editable in the HTML source. Do not rely on images, icons, or external fonts.
- Include print-friendly styling for the workout and weekly tracker sections.
- Clearly label estimates, experiments, optional items, and safety boundaries.
